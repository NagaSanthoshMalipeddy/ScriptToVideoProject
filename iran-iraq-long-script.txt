Imagine a war that lasts…

eight years.

Two neighboring countries.

Millions of people affected.

Cities bombed.

Oil facilities attacked.

And a conflict that eventually became one of the longest and bloodiest conventional wars of the 20th century.

This was the Iran–Iraq War.

But why did Iraq invade Iran?

Why did the war continue for eight years?

And how did it finally end?

Let's go back to 1980.

To understand the war, look at the map.

Here is Iraq.

And directly to its east…

Iran.

The two countries shared a long and strategically important border.

One particularly important area was the Shatt al-Arab waterway, where the Tigris and Euphrates rivers meet before flowing toward the Persian Gulf.

The border and control of this waterway had been disputed for years.

But there was another major development.

In 1979, Iran experienced the Islamic Revolution.

The monarchy was overthrown, and a new Islamic Republic was established under Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini.

The revolution dramatically changed Iran's government and its relationship with neighboring countries.

Iraq's leader, Saddam Hussein, saw Iran as being politically unsettled.

He also saw an opportunity to strengthen Iraq's position in the region.

Tensions rapidly increased.

And then…

Iraq made its move.

September 22, 1980.

Look at the map.

The Iraqi flag appears on the western side.

Then…

it starts moving east.

Iraqi forces launched a large-scale invasion of Iran.

Multiple Iraqi units crossed the border.

Iraq attacked targets across western Iran and launched air strikes against Iranian military and infrastructure targets.

The expectation was that Iran's political turmoil and military problems would make it vulnerable.

Initially, Iraq made territorial gains.

But the war did not go according to plan.

Iran began mobilizing its military and revolutionary forces.

And instead of collapsing…

Iran fought back.

The Iraqi advance slowed.

Then it stopped.

And eventually…

the direction of the war began to change.

The Iranian flag begins moving west.

Iran launched counteroffensives and gradually recovered much of the territory Iraq had captured.

By 1982, Iranian forces had pushed Iraqi forces out of most of the Iranian territory they had occupied.

At this point, Iraq had an opportunity to end the conflict.

But the war continued.

Iran demanded that Iraq accept responsibility for the invasion and sought other conditions before a permanent settlement.

Iranian forces then crossed into Iraqi territory.

And the war changed completely.

Instead of a short invasion…

it became a long war of attrition.

Both sides were now trying to exhaust the other.

And neither side could easily achieve a decisive victory.

1982.

1983.

1984.

1985.

The front line moves…

then moves back.

Neither side achieves a decisive breakthrough.

The conflict increasingly became a war of attrition.

Both countries launched attacks against military positions.

But the war wasn't limited to the battlefield.

Oil became extremely important.

Iran and Iraq depended heavily on oil revenues.

So oil facilities and shipping became targets.

This expanded the conflict into the Persian Gulf.

Commercial shipping was attacked.

Oil tankers came under threat.

And other countries became increasingly concerned about the security of the Gulf.

The war was no longer just an Iran-Iraq problem.

It was becoming an international crisis.

One of the darkest aspects of the Iran-Iraq War was the use of chemical weapons.

Chemical weapons were used during the conflict, and Iraq's use became particularly significant.

The attacks caused severe injuries and deaths.

One of the most notorious examples came in 1988, when the Kurdish town of Halabja was attacked with chemical agents during the final phase of the war.

This period demonstrated how devastating the conflict had become.

The war was no longer about quickly capturing territory.

It had become a prolonged struggle with enormous human costs.

Meanwhile…

neither Iran nor Iraq was able to secure a decisive victory.

By the late 1980s, both countries were exhausted.

The war had lasted for years.

The economy of both countries had suffered.

Cities and infrastructure had been damaged.

And huge numbers of people had been killed, wounded, displaced, or otherwise affected.

The United Nations pushed for a ceasefire.

The UN Security Council adopted Resolution 598, calling for a ceasefire and other steps toward ending the conflict.

Eventually, Iran accepted the resolution.

Iraq also accepted it.

And after eight years of war…

the fighting finally stopped.

August 20, 1988.

The ceasefire came into effect.

On the map…

the Iraqi flag stops.

The Iranian flag stops.

The front line freezes.

After nearly eight years of fighting…

the guns finally became silent.

There was no dramatic conquest of Iran.

There was no decisive Iraqi victory.

And Iran did not conquer Iraq.

The war ended through a ceasefire rather than a clear military victory for either side.

The two countries had fought for years…

only to return broadly to the pre-war international border framework.

The Iran-Iraq War changed the Middle East permanently.

It showed how quickly a regional dispute could become an enormous international crisis.

It devastated both countries.

It drew in outside powers.

It threatened one of the world's most important oil-producing regions.

And it left behind millions of people affected by the conflict.

The war began with an Iraqi invasion in 1980…

and ended with a ceasefire in 1988.

Eight years.

Two countries.

One devastating war.

And no clear winner.

This was the Iran–Iraq War.
