Imagine waking up one morning…

and suddenly, an army from the neighboring country is crossing your border.

Within just three days, the capital city falls.

Then another superpower enters the war.

Then another.

And suddenly, a conflict between two parts of one country becomes a major Cold War confrontation involving the United States, China, the Soviet Union, and the United Nations.

This was the Korean War.

But how did North Korea and South Korea end up fighting each other?

And why, more than 70 years later, are they still technically at war?

Let's go back to the beginning.

Before North Korea and South Korea existed as separate countries, there was one Korea.

For decades, Korea was under Japanese rule.

But everything changed when World War II ended in 1945.

Japan surrendered, and Korea was liberated.

But then came the Cold War.

The United States and the Soviet Union divided the Korean Peninsula into two occupation zones.

The dividing line was the 38th parallel.

The Soviet Union supported the establishment of a communist government in the north.

The United States supported a separate government in the south.

And in 1948, two different Korean states emerged.

In the north was the Democratic People's Republic of Korea, led by Kim Il Sung.

In the south was the Republic of Korea, led by Syngman Rhee.

Both governments claimed to represent Korea.

And both wanted Korea unified under their own system.

The stage was set.

Now imagine the map of the Korean Peninsula.

North Korea is at the top.

South Korea is at the bottom.

And right in between them…

the 38th parallel.

Then comes the date that changed Korean history.

June 25, 1950.

North Korean forces crossed the 38th parallel and launched a massive invasion of South Korea.

On the map, you can see the North Korean flag moving south.

The North Korean army advanced rapidly.

South Korean forces were caught off guard and began retreating.

And the first major target was the South Korean capital…

Seoul.

Within just a few days, Seoul fell to North Korean forces.

The South Korean government and its forces were pushed farther and farther south.

The invasion had completely changed the situation.

But North Korea had made one huge calculation…

It expected the conflict to remain limited.

Instead, the United States and the United Nations entered the war.

The invasion immediately became an international crisis.

The United Nations Security Council called for North Korea to stop fighting and withdraw.

When North Korea continued its advance, the UN authorized military assistance for South Korea.

The United States became the main contributor to the UN force.

American troops, along with forces from other countries, were sent to Korea.

General Douglas MacArthur was placed in command of the UN forces.

But the situation was still extremely difficult.

North Korean troops continued pushing south.

By August 1950, UN and South Korean forces had been pushed into a defensive position around the southeastern corner of the peninsula.

This became known as the Pusan Perimeter.

At one point, the UN forces controlled only a relatively small part of South Korea.

It looked like North Korea might actually win the war.

But then…

MacArthur made a risky move.

Instead of simply fighting the North Korean army head-on…

UN forces launched a surprise amphibious landing at Incheon, near Seoul.

The operation took place on September 15, 1950.

The plan worked.

North Korean supply lines were disrupted.

UN forces pushed toward Seoul.

And by the end of September, Seoul had been recaptured.

Now the entire war changed direction.

The North Korean army began retreating.

And UN and South Korean forces crossed the 38th parallel and moved into North Korea.

Then they captured Pyongyang, the North Korean capital.

At this point, the war was no longer simply about defending South Korea.

The UN forces were moving deeper into North Korea.

And they were getting closer and closer to the Chinese border.

That was when China decided to enter the war.

Look at the map again.

North Korea is here.

China is directly above it.

And the border between them is the Yalu River.

As UN forces moved north toward the Chinese border, Chinese leaders became increasingly concerned about the fighting reaching their territory.

Then, in late 1950, Chinese forces entered the Korean War.

Now the battlefield had completely changed.

Chinese forces launched major attacks against UN and South Korean troops.

The UN forces were pushed back.

And in early 1951, Seoul was captured by communist forces again.

But the fighting didn't stop there.

UN forces regrouped and eventually retook Seoul.

The front moved north again.

And then south again.

And then north again.

The war had become a brutal back-and-forth struggle.

By the middle of 1951, the front line had stabilized roughly around the 38th parallel.

And now came the strangest phase of the war.

The armies were still fighting…

but neither side could achieve a decisive victory.

For the next two years, fighting continued.

But the front line barely moved compared with the enormous advances and retreats of 1950.

At the same time, negotiations for an armistice began.

But reaching an agreement was incredibly difficult.

One major issue involved prisoners of war and whether they should be forced to return to their original side.

Meanwhile, soldiers continued fighting along the front.

Artillery.

Air attacks.

Infantry battles.

And enormous human losses.

The Korean War became one of the bloodiest conflicts of the early Cold War.

Millions of people died, including civilians.

And the Korean Peninsula was devastated.

But eventually, after years of fighting and negotiations…

an agreement was finally reached.

July 27, 1953.

An armistice was signed.

The fighting stopped.

A new boundary was established near the 38th parallel.

And a Demilitarized Zone, or DMZ, was created between North and South Korea.

But here's the shocking part.

This was not a peace treaty.

It was an armistice — an agreement to stop the fighting.

North Korea and South Korea never signed a permanent peace treaty ending the war.

So the Korean Peninsula remained divided.

North Korea remained a communist state.

South Korea remained a separate state aligned with the United States and its allies.

And the DMZ became one of the most heavily militarized borders in the world.

So what started as a conflict between two governments claiming the same peninsula…

became an international Cold War confrontation.

North Korea invaded South Korea.

The United Nations and the United States intervened.

UN forces pushed into North Korea.

China entered the war.

The front line moved back and forth.

And after three years…

the guns finally went silent.

But the conflict never received a final peace treaty.

That's why the Korean Peninsula remains divided to this day.

Two countries.

One peninsula.

One heavily guarded border.

And a war that technically never received a permanent peace settlement.

This was the Korean War.
